Before I worked on construction data I spent a semester supervising execution on industrial structures, taking my own measurements and preparing bills of quantities from them.

I expected it to be administrative. It turned out to be the most useful engineering education I got, and it changed how I read every model I have looked at since.

## The drawing is a proposal

In a lecture hall, a drawing is the answer. You analyse what it shows, you check it against a code, and the question is closed.

On a site the drawing is the opening position. The steel that arrived is a slightly different section because that is what the supplier had. The opening moved two hundred millimetres because a service had to pass. The pour stopped short of the intended joint because the truck was late and the concrete would not have been workable.

None of these are failures. Every one of them is a reasonable decision made by somebody solving a real constraint. But each one puts distance between the drawing and the structure, and nobody records that distance in a form an analyst can later use.

When you are the one measuring for the bill, you cannot avoid noticing. You are standing there with a tape, and the number you write down is the number that gets paid. Getting it right requires you to look at what exists rather than at what was specified.

## Quantities are where disagreement becomes explicit

A running account bill is an unusually honest document, because two parties with opposing incentives both have to sign it.

That makes it a good place to learn what is actually ambiguous in a design. Ambiguity that nobody notices in a drawing review becomes a conversation the moment somebody has to be paid for it. Where does this element start and the next one end. Is this excavation or is it rock. Does this count as one operation or two.

I learned more about how designs are actually interpreted from those conversations than from any specification I read. The specification tells you what was intended. The bill tells you what was understood.

## Why this connects to everything I do now

My work is comparing what was planned against what exists. The BOQ semester is the reason I take the second half of that sentence seriously.

It is easy, working with models and capture data, to slip into treating the model as ground truth and any discrepancy as a data problem. The model is not ground truth. It is the same proposal the drawing was, expressed in three dimensions, and the building on site has been diverging from it since the first day of work in ways that are mostly sensible and almost entirely unrecorded.

The interesting engineering is in the divergence. How much of it can a schedule absorb before a date moves. Which deviations are cosmetic and which change how a following trade has to work. Whether a difference between model and scan is an error in the capture, an error in the model, or a decision somebody made at seven in the morning to keep the job moving.

You cannot answer any of that from the model alone. You need to have been the person with the tape at some point, or at least to remember that such a person exists.

## The part I did not expect

My thesis was on plates that are not perfectly flat, and how much that imperfection changes the load at which they buckle. The whole subject is what a small departure from the ideal does to the behaviour of the real thing.

I did not connect the two at the time. I was doing finite element work in one part of my life and writing measurements into a bill in another, and it took a while to notice they were the same preoccupation. Ideal geometry is a convenience. Everything interesting happens in the gap between it and what got built.

That is not a lesson I got from a course. I got it standing on a slab with a tape measure, arguing about where an element ended.
