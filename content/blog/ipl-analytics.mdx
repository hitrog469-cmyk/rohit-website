The 2024 IPL final. KKR vs SRH. KKR win comfortably. Everyone around me is watching the wickets fall and the boundaries go. I'm watching the wagon wheel, the pitch map, the economy rate trend, and a spreadsheet I've been updating since February.

My cousin looked at me and said: *"Brother, you watch cricket like you're doing homework."*

He's not wrong. And I don't care.

---

## How It Started

It started with a frustration. During IPL 2022, everyone was arguing about which team had the best death bowling attack. "MI has Bumrah." "CSK has Bravo." "RCB has... well."

But nobody had *numbers*. It was all vibes and highlight reels.

So I pulled the [CricSheet](https://cricsheet.org/) dataset — ball-by-ball data going back to 2008 — and started building.

---

## What the Data Actually Shows

Here are three things that changed how I watch IPL, backed by data:

### 1. The Powerplay Is Underrated

Most cricket analysis focuses on the death overs (16–20). The "finisher" narrative. But the data shows something different: **teams that post high powerplay scores win disproportionately often**.

In IPL 2022–2024, teams that scored 50+ in the powerplay won 71% of their matches. Teams that scored 45 or below won only 38%.

The powerplay isn't just setup. It's the match.

Why? Because the fielding restrictions create a compounding effect. An aggressive powerplay puts the fielding side under pressure, which affects their mid-overs bowling strategy, which opens up the death overs. Cricket is a sequential game. The first six overs set the initial conditions.

As a structural engineer, I recognize this immediately. Initial conditions determine the solution. Boundary conditions at t=0 matter more than anything that happens in the middle.

### 2. Economy Rate in Overs 7–15 Is the Most Predictive Single Metric

Death bowling gets all the glory. But the boring middle overs — 7 to 15 — contain the most predictive signal for match outcomes.

I ran a regression on winning probability against various bowling metrics. The mid-over economy rate (overs 7–15) had the highest correlation with winning percentage of any single metric I tested — higher than death bowling economy, higher than powerplay wickets, higher than anything.

Teams that hold opponents to ≤7.5 runs/over in the middle overs win 68% of the time. Teams that concede ≥9 runs/over in those overs win only 29%.

This tells you something about the teams that consistently win IPL titles. They're not just buying flashy death bowlers. They're building deep bowling attacks that can control the middle.

### 3. Strike Rate in Overs 13–17 Matters More Than Overall Strike Rate

Here's the counterintuitive one. A batter with an overall strike rate of 145 sounds better than one with 130. But if the 145 SR batter achieves that mostly in the powerplay and drops to 110 in overs 13–17, they're less valuable in a chase than a batter who sustains 135+ throughout.

I call this the "acceleration profile" — the shape of a batter's strike rate curve across a typical innings. The batters with the best acceleration profiles (steady 130s through the middle, then 160+ in the death) are consistently more valuable than one-dimensional big-hitters.

Rohit Sharma's profile, by the way, is nearly perfect. Consistent through the middle, devastating in the death.

## Building the Model

I built a basic match outcome predictor using:

- **Features:** Powerplay score, powerplay wickets, mid-overs economy (7–15), current run rate vs required run rate at overs 10, 15
- **Model:** Gradient boosted trees (XGBoost) trained on 2015–2023 IPL data
- **Validation:** Tested on 2024 IPL — 74% accuracy for in-match prediction at the 15-over mark

74% isn't perfect. Cricket is gloriously unpredictable. But it's well above the 50% baseline and well above most commentary panels, who I've tracked for fun.

The biggest improvements came from adding **fielding quality metrics** (misfields, dropped catches) and **pitch condition proxies** (surface data, dew factor). The model doesn't know about injuries or team selection changes — that's where the human eye still beats the algorithm.

## What This Means for How I Watch

I watch cricket differently now. Not better, necessarily — just differently.

When a team is 45/2 in the powerplay, I'm not thinking "good start." I'm thinking: *what's the acceleration required to reach a competitive total, and does the remaining batting order have the profiles to deliver it?*

When a spinner is bowling in overs 7–9 and going for 8 an over, I'm immediately flagging it as a match-deciding passage — not because of the runs, but because of the momentum it gives the batting team for the death.

My cousin thinks I've ruined cricket for myself. I think I've added a layer. Like knowing music theory — it doesn't make the song less beautiful. It just lets you hear more of what's happening.

## A Note on Medium-Pace Bowling

I'm a medium-pace bowler. I have been since I was 14. I bowl in the 115–120 km/h range, which is the most brutally data-exposed range in cricket.

The numbers are brutal for medium-pacers in T20. Economy rates above 9 in the death. Batters who can play both pace and spin hunt medium-pace like they're reading a menu.

But the data also shows something that keeps me bowling: **variation and accuracy at medium pace beat raw pace without control every time.** A 115 km/h delivery that hits the seam and nips back beats a 130 km/h full toss every day.

I'm still working on the seam. The pitch is the lab.

**हर हर महादेव** — and may your economy rate never exceed 8.
