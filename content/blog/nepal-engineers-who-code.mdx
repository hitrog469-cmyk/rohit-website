Kathmandu sits on a sedimentary basin with one of the worst seismic amplification profiles in South Asia. In 2015, a 7.8 magnitude earthquake killed nearly 9,000 people and destroyed over 600,000 structures.

The geological data that predicted this had existed for decades.

---

## The Data Is There. The Tools Aren't.

Nepal has been collecting earthquake data since the 1970s. The Department of Mines and Geology has sensors all over the country. The engineering departments at IOE Pulchowk have been running research programs for years.

But ask an engineer working in the field what software they use to process field data, and the answer is almost always **Excel**.

Not because Excel is the right tool. Because it's the one they know.

I don't blame them. The civil engineering curriculum in Nepal — and honestly, across most of South Asia — treats coding as something other people do. The "soft skills" section. The optional workshop at the end of semester.

Meanwhile, a 22-year-old data science graduate in Bangalore is building flood prediction models from satellite imagery. A PhD student in Singapore is running real-time structural health monitoring on bridges using machine learning. The tools exist. The gap is whether engineers know how to use them.

---

## What I Learned at NIT Rourkela

I went to NIT Rourkela for a reason: it's one of the best engineering institutions in India, and I wanted to see what world-class looked like.

What I found is that the best students there weren't just engineers or just coders. They were both. The ones who made the most impact — in research, in internships, in hackathons — were the people who could build an ABAQUS model **and** write the Python script that automated the parametric study **and** visualize the results in a way non-engineers could understand.

I built the FG-GRC Simulator because I was tired of manually adjusting ABAQUS input files for each parametric variation. What started as a quality-of-life tool became a proper web application that lets you input GPL weight fraction, boundary conditions, and plate geometry — and get buckling load results in under a second.

That's what coding does for engineering. It takes something that took weeks and makes it available instantly.

## The Infrastructure Gap

Nepal has **~80,000 bridges**, according to the Department of Roads. Most have no structural monitoring. When a bridge fails — and several do every year during monsoon season — the failure is always "sudden" and always "unexpected."

It's not unexpected. It's uninstrumented.

Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) systems — networks of sensors that continuously measure deflection, vibration, strain, and corrosion — cost less than USD 50,000 for a medium-span bridge. That's a fraction of the bridge's construction cost. The data these systems generate can predict failure years in advance.

But SHM data is useless without engineers who can process it. And right now, Nepal doesn't have enough of those.

---

## What This Looks Like in Practice

I built the SHM Dashboard project partly as a proof of concept for what this could look like. Real-time sensor ingestion, anomaly detection, 3D visualization of stress distribution. It's not production-ready for Nepali infrastructure — but the point is that a 24-year-old with no institutional budget can build a working prototype in a few months.

Imagine what a team of ten engineers with institutional backing could do.

---

## What Needs to Change

**In education:** Python or any scripting language should be a core part of civil engineering curricula. Not a special elective. Not the computer science department's problem. Core.

**In practice:** Engineering firms in Nepal should stop treating computation as a cost center and start treating it as a competitive advantage. The firms that automate their analysis pipelines will do more work, faster, with fewer errors.

**In culture:** We need to stop treating "engineers who code" as an anomaly or a career hybrid. It's just engineering done well in 2026.

---

## A Note to Nepali Engineering Students

If you're reading this and you're at IOE, KU, or any engineering college in Nepal: learn to code. Not because it's trendy. Because the problems Nepal needs to solve — disaster resilience, infrastructure monitoring, smart cities, climate adaptation — all require engineers who can build tools, not just use them.

The data is there. The need is there. The tools are open-source and free.

The only thing missing is you.
