A late finish is an outcome. A delay is a mechanism. Reporting usually conflates them, and by the time the conflation is noticed the useful window has closed.

The distinction matters because the same late finish can arrive by completely different routes, and the routes need opposite responses.

## Three ways to arrive at the same date

Suppose an activity that should have closed in week twelve closes in week sixteen.

It might have started on time and run slowly. That is a productivity problem, and the fix is about resource, method or workface.

It might have run at exactly the planned rate but started four weeks late because its predecessor was not ready. That is not a problem with this activity at all. Adding people to it would waste money and change nothing.

Or it might have started on time, run at the planned rate, and been interrupted twice by something that had nothing to do with either. That is a disruption problem, and it will recur next month unless somebody addresses the cause rather than the symptom.

Every one of those shows up in a monthly report as four weeks late. Only the first is fixed by pushing harder on the activity that is late.

## Rate and start date, kept separate

The practical version of this is to stop reporting a single completion number and instead track two things about every activity: when it actually became able to start, and what rate it has run at since.

Those two together explain nearly every late finish I have looked at. They also fail in different, recognisable ways.

A start date that keeps slipping while rates hold steady means the problem lives upstream, in sequencing or in handover between trades. A rate that keeps falling while start dates hold means something is wrong in the work itself, or in the conditions it is being done under. Both drifting at once usually means the plan was never achievable and has been absorbing that quietly for months.

Line of balance shows this well, because it plots exactly those two quantities. The intercept is when you started. The slope is how fast you are going. A late finish is just where the line crosses a date, and looking only at the crossing throws away everything that explains it.

## Forecasting is a claim about which mechanism continues

This is where forecasting gets genuinely difficult, and where I think the honest answer is less confident than most reporting sounds.

To forecast, you have to assume something about the future. Usually the assumption is that the recent rate continues. Sometimes that is right. But if the recent rate was depressed because a specific zone was congested, and that zone is about to clear, extrapolating it forward is guaranteed to be wrong in a predictable direction.

So a forecast is not really a projection of a number. It is a claim about which mechanism is going to keep operating. Stating that claim out loud tends to improve the conversation considerably, because the people on site usually know whether it is true, and they will tell you if you have given them something specific to disagree with.

"We will finish in week nineteen" invites a yes or no. "We will finish in week nineteen if the current pour cycle holds and the east zone frees up on schedule" invites somebody to say that the east zone will not free up, which is the information you actually wanted.

## What I have not solved

Separating the mechanisms after the fact is tractable. Doing it early enough to act is harder, because early in an activity you have very little rate data and the noise is large.

There is a real question about how much history you need before a change in rate is a signal rather than variation, and I suspect the answer is different for different trades and different locations. I have not found a good general treatment of it. That is one of the things I would like time and a proper research setting to work on.

For now the honest position is that a slope needs a few points before it means anything, that everybody wants an answer before those points exist, and that saying so plainly is better than producing a confident number that was never supported.
