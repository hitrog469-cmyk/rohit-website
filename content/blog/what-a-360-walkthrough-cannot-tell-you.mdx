A camera walked through a floor last week and produced a complete record of every surface it could see. That record is genuinely useful, and it is also less than people assume.

The gap is not accuracy. Registration against the model is good, and getting better. The gap is that a walkthrough answers a question about appearance, and almost every question a project actually needs answered is about readiness.

## Visible is not the same as complete

A wall can look finished from every angle the camera passed and still not be complete in the sense the schedule means. The blockwork is up but the joints have not cured. The conduit is in but has not been inspected. The plaster is on but the surface has not been signed off, so the painter cannot start, so the activity that the model reads as done is holding up its successor anyway.

Automated inference has to make a decision about that wall from what the image shows. It usually decides correctly. When it decides incorrectly, the error is rarely random. It is systematically optimistic, because the visible state of construction runs ahead of the contractual state of construction almost everywhere.

That bias matters more than the error rate. A model that is wrong in both directions averages out across a floor. A model that is wrong in one direction accumulates.

## Occlusion is a scheduling problem, not a camera problem

The obvious limitation is that a camera cannot see what is covered. The less obvious one is that what gets covered is not random either.

Services get boxed in. Slabs get loaded with material. A zone gets handed to a trade who fills it with their own equipment for three weeks. In each case the thing you most want to verify has become the thing you cannot see, and it became invisible precisely because work moved forward.

So capture quality degrades exactly where progress is fastest. Any honest reading of a walkthrough has to account for that, and the accounting is currently done by people who know the site, not by the pipeline.

## The questions that need a person

There is a category of judgment that I have not seen a capture pipeline handle, and I am not sure it should.

Whether a partially complete condition is acceptable to the following trade. Whether a deviation is cosmetic or structural. Whether the reason a zone is empty is that it is finished, or that it was abandoned this morning because of something nobody has reported yet. Whether the sequence being followed on site is the sequence in the plan, or a sensible field decision that the plan has not caught up with.

Each of those needs somebody who has stood in the room. The interesting design question is not how to remove that person. It is where exactly to place them so their attention lands on the cases that need it, rather than on the ninety percent the pipeline already handled correctly.

## What I actually do with a walkthrough

I treat it as strong evidence about geometry and weak evidence about status.

Geometry it is excellent at. Is the wall in the right place, is the slab at the right level, does the as-built match the model within tolerance. Those are questions about surfaces, and surfaces are what it measured.

Status it can only propose. Progress claims that come out of a walkthrough get checked against sequence logic, against what the following trades are reporting, and against whether the claimed completion would have been physically possible given what the previous capture showed.

None of that makes the capture less valuable. It makes it a measurement rather than an answer, which is the correct thing for a measurement to be.

The failure mode I watch for is not the pipeline being wrong. It is the pipeline being trusted in the register where it is weakest, because it was so obviously right in the register where it is strong.
